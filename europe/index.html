Government Jobs in Europe for Foreigners

Europe is not one jobs market but thirty-one — and there is no single "European work visa". This hub separates the two doors that matter: EURES, which connects foreign workers to national public employers, and EPSO, which recruits the EU institutions' own staff from EU citizens only — plus the named national schemes worth knowing.

Can non-EU citizens get government jobs in Europe?

EURES, the European Commission's job-mobility portal, is fed by public employment services in 31 countries. Employers post there specifically to recruit across borders, and non-EU workers can be hired where a national work-permit route exists — job offer first, then the permit. EURES advisers explain each country's rules for free. EPSO (EU Careers) runs the competitions for permanent jobs in the EU institutions: applicants must be citizens of an EU member state and know at least two EU languages. There is no sponsorship route into EPSO.

What is the EU Blue Card?

The EU Blue Card is the shared work-and-residence permit for highly qualified non-EU workers, available in most member states. It requires a recognised degree (or qualifying experience in some countries), a concrete job offer in a skilled role, and a salary above the national threshold, which each country sets itself.

Germany's Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte)

Germany's Opportunity Card (Chancenkarte) is a points-based job-seeker route: qualified non-EU workers can enter Germany for up to a year to look for a job, instead of needing an offer before they travel. Points are awarded for qualifications, language skills, experience and age. Applications run through the German authorities — never through paid agents.

Ireland's Critical Skills Employment Permit

Ireland — an EURES country where English is the working language — targets shortage occupations through its Critical Skills Employment Permit, aimed at highly skilled roles such as health, engineering and technology professionals with a genuine job offer from an Irish employer.

Language and recognition

Two things decide most European routes. Qualification recognition: regulated professions (health, teaching, engineering) must have their qualifications recognised in the destination country — Anerkennung in Germany, for example — and it can take months. Language: most public-facing roles require the local language at roughly B1–B2 level, and health roles require it for patient safety.
